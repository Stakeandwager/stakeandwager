# Running a Table

**A working guide to the Stakeandwager Protocol v1.0**

The protocol says what the rules are. This says how to actually run one.

---

## Before you open anything

**Work out what you've already spent.** Not what you plan to spend — what is
gone. Hours worked, money paid, a prototype that exists, access you hold. If you
can't name it in numbers, you are not ready to open a table.

**Then write the outcome, and try to break it.** Show it to somebody and ask:
*could this come out no?* If they hesitate, it isn't specific enough. The
sentence that survives that question is your milestone.

Most people fail here, and it is the useful failure. Discovering that you cannot
say what would prove you wrong is worth more than a table that never settles.

**Pick your verification before you need it.** If a public trace exists — a
filing, a commit, a receipt, a weighbridge slip — use it. If not, self-report and
say so. Do not leave it open.

**Set a date you would be embarrassed to miss.** Close enough to matter, far
enough to be possible. Two weeks is often better than two months; a short table
that resolves teaches you more than a long one that drifts.

---

## Writing the ask

The ask is the part people actually read. Three rules.

**Bounded.** *Thirty minutes reviewing the architecture* — not *some help with
the backend*.

**No larger than your stake.** You cannot ask for two hundred hours against your
own six.

**Specific about who.** *An ML engineer* rather than *anyone technical*. A named
kind of person feels chosen; an open call feels like a broadcast.

---

## Finding people

**Approach individuals, not audiences.** A post to your whole network cannot tell
you who ignored it or why. Fifteen personal messages can.

**Say why them.** One line explaining why you picked this person is the single
biggest predictor of a reply. If you can't write that line, they are the wrong
person to ask.

**Lead with the stake, not the request.** *I've put X down; I'm asking for Y* is
a different message from *could you help me with something*.

**Don't chase.** A reply produced by a reminder is solicited, and solicited data
is the thing the protocol is trying to see past. Send once. Record the silence.

**Log every send.** Who, when, how, and the reason-you line. Note refusals in
their own words — the people who say no will teach you more than the people who
say yes.

---

## While the table is open

**Record four things separately, never as one number.**

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Committed | They agreed to a specific thing |
| Completed | They actually delivered it |
| Solicited / organic | Did you ask, or did they arrive |
| Escalated | Did they voluntarily do more, unprompted |

**Escalation has a strict definition.** *I'll spend another hour on this* is not
escalation. *Here is the hour* is. Split what people offer into two kinds:

- **Stated intention** — future availability, no deliverable, no date
- **Defined deliverable** — a specific thing, with a date attached

Only the second counts. And log the **exact sentence, verbatim**, next to the
tick — so the judgment can be inspected later instead of remembered
sympathetically.

**Do not adjust the table.** Not the date, not the milestone, not the ask. If it
was badly specified, that is a finding. Let it resolve and write a better
successor.

---

## Resolving

On the date, and not before:

**Establish the outcome** at whatever tier you chose. Tier 1: state it and mark
it self-reported. Tier 2: link the trace. Tier 3: name the attestor and the date.

**Publish it whichever way it goes.** A no is published as a no, in the same
place and the same size as a yes would have been.

**Tell everyone who contributed.** One message: what happened, what the answers
had in common, and what changed because of theirs. This is the thing you promised
when you asked, and it is what makes a second table possible.

**Then stop.** Resolution is not the moment to explain yourself.

---

## Afterwards

**If you want to say why, say it as narrative.** Attributed, dated, marked as
your account and not as evidence. Then leave it alone — an explanation revised
later is worthless.

**If you believe your explanation, test it.** That is what succession is for. New
table, new stake, the explanation as the proposition, a date. If you were right,
the result will say so more convincingly than any post-mortem.

**If you don't test it, that shows too.** A chain where the account was never put
at risk reads differently from one where it was, and readers can see that
without being told.

---

## Common failures

**Opening empty.** A table with an ask and no stake. The rule catches it, but the
instinct behind it — *let me see if there's interest first* — is the one to
notice in yourself.

**An outcome that can't lose.** *Make good progress on the redesign* passes as a
sentence and fails as a milestone.

**Verification decided late.** Deciding at the deadline how you'll prove it is
moving the goalposts with extra steps.

**Counting interest as commitment.** Enthusiastic replies are not commitments.
Commitments have a thing and a date.

**Chasing, then counting.** Three reminders and a reply is not evidence about the
mechanism. It is evidence about your persistence.

**Explaining instead of succeeding.** The long post-mortem, no second table. The
protocol has a specific answer: put it back on the table.

---

## First table checklist

- [ ] Stake named in numbers, already spent
- [ ] Proposition in one sentence
- [ ] Outcome that could come out **no**
- [ ] Deadline set, short enough to resolve
- [ ] Ask bounded, and smaller than the stake
- [ ] Verification tier chosen **now**
- [ ] Terms stated if the ask is above advice class
- [ ] Stop condition written down
- [ ] Fifteen people, named, with a reason for each
- [ ] A place the result will be published
